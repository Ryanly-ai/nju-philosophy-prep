## 📖 今日短文（考研英一阅读风格 · ~275 词）

### The Case for Reforming Peer Review

For decades, the academic **journal** has stood at the center of scientific **research**, with **peer** review serving as its quality gate. Yet growing **evidence** now suggests that this **particular** **section** of the publishing pipeline is in urgent need of **reform**.

The traditional model **defines** validity narrowly: a manuscript is sent to two or three anonymous experts, whose **comprehension** of the **subject** matter determines whether the work proceeds. But as scholarship has moved into the **digital** era, the volume of submissions has surged. A 2023 **commission** convened by the U.S. **Federal** Office of Science and Technology Policy reported that reviewers now must **address** dozens of dense **paragraphs** per week — a load that, on its own findings, leaves little room for careful reading.

**Congress** held a brief hearing on the matter last year. Witnesses described how a single misjudged **paragraph** can derail a researcher's career. Even the **court** of public opinion has noticed: when prominent papers are retracted, the remaining authors are seldom in **accord** with the journal's verdict, and disputes occasionally reach actual courts.

Reform proposals fall into two camps. The first would publish reviews openly, filling the **blank** that anonymity has long maintained. The second would mandate a structured training **course** for reviewers — one teaching them how to weigh evidence, **define** methodological standards, and recognize when a topic lies outside their expertise. Whether either path succeeds, the academic community broadly agrees: peer review, in its current form, no longer commands the trust it once did.
