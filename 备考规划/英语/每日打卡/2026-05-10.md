## 📖 今日短文（考研英一阅读风格 · ~280 词）

### Why Digital Education Often Resembles a Market

Modern online education often **resembles** a crowded market more than a quiet classroom. Prices **fluctuate**, platforms compete for attention, and companies sometimes **manipulate** users by turning learning into a game of points and badges. For students, the real challenge is to **distinguish** useful tools from merely **artificial** excitement.

A well-designed course can **attract** millions, even **billion**-dollar **campaign** funding, yet popularity does not prove educational value. Many platforms claim to **care** about disadvantaged learners, but they often rely on **celebrity** teachers, bright visual **display**, and the **chance** that anxious parents will pay quickly. The central **character** of this industry is not always knowledge; sometimes it is speed, pressure, and cheap confidence.

This does not mean digital education is simply **cheap** or harmful. Its promise is real, especially when it helps students handle **complex** materials at their own pace. A public **constitution** for online learning is impossible, but clearer rules may prevent a future **crisis** in which schools cannot **cure** the gap between access and achievement. Policymakers may need to **cut** empty advertising, require evidence of progress, and protect vulnerable families from exaggerated claims.

For individual learners, the lesson is practical. A tool should be judged by whether it reduces a **difficult** task into manageable steps, not by whether it looks new, fashionable, or particular enough to impress others. In education, as in public policy, the most useful reform is often the least dramatic one.
